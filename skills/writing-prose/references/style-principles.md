# Style Principles Reference

This reference distills practical prose guidance from the public-domain 1918 edition of William Strunk Jr.'s The Elements of Style and from general editorial practice. It does not reproduce copyrighted later editions.

## Composition

- Choose a design and stick to it. Readers should sense a deliberate order.
- Make the paragraph the unit of composition. Each paragraph should advance one main idea or function.
- Begin and end with strength. Put crucial material in positions readers notice.
- Use transitions to show relation: addition, contrast, cause, concession, sequence, example, conclusion.
- Omit digressions unless they pay rent.

## Sentences

- Prefer simple sentence architecture for complex ideas: clear subject, strong verb, necessary object or complement.
- Use active voice by default: "The team shipped the release" is usually stronger than "The release was shipped." Use passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately deemphasized.
- Put modifiers next to what they modify.
- Keep coordinate ideas in coordinate form: matching grammar helps readers compare ideas.
- Avoid stringing together many loose clauses with "and," "but," or "which." Split or subordinate.
- Vary sentence length. Short sentences add force after longer exposition.

## Words

- Cut needless words: empty openers, redundant pairs, inflated phrases, repeated qualifiers, and explanatory padding.
- Replace vague nouns with concrete ones when precision matters.
- Replace weak verb phrases with verbs: "make a decision" -> "decide"; "give consideration to" -> "consider."
- Avoid fashionable jargon, clichés, and business fog unless they are the audience's shared shorthand.
- Use technical terms when they make the prose more precise for the intended reader.
- Avoid overstatement. Exact claims build trust.

## Common fixes

- Before: "It is important to note that we are in a position to begin implementation."
  After: "We can begin implementation."
- Before: "There are several reasons why the proposal is risky."
  After: "The proposal is risky for three reasons."
- Before: "The decision was made by the committee."
  After: "The committee decided."
- Before: "Due to the fact that demand increased, we hired more support staff."
  After: "Because demand increased, we hired more support staff."

## When not to cut

Do not make prose terse at the expense of:

- accuracy or legal nuance;
- warmth in sensitive messages;
- necessary context for unfamiliar readers;
- accessibility for non-experts;
- rhythm, emphasis, or genre conventions.

## Review questions

Ask these in order:

1. What should the reader know, feel, or do after reading?
2. Does the opening make that direction clear?
3. What can be removed because it repeats, delays, or dilutes the point?
4. Which nouns and verbs carry the sentence? Are they precise?
5. Where might a reader stumble, object, or ask "so what?"
6. Is the ending decisive?
