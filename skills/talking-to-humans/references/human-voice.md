# Normal words and the human voice

These notes paraphrase two essays from gilest.org. They are not copies of the essays.

## Use the words normal people would use

Source: [Use the words normal people would use](https://gilest.org/notes/normal-words.html), 10 August 2015.

The essay describes a sign at the Eden Project that asks visitors not to stand on a particular wooden part of an exhibit. Its wording works because it is courteous, concrete, and what someone might actually say. An abrupt prohibition would sound aggressive; a vague instruction would leave people unsure where not to stand.

The author also describes helping people write by talking through their ideas first. People often explain themselves clearly in conversation, then become less clear when they switch into writing mode. The conversation supplies the words the writing needs.

Apply this to agent messages:

- Imagine explaining the work to someone who has not been following every step.
- Use the words you would say to them, rather than compressed labels from your working notes.
- Make the reference concrete. Ordinary words are not enough if the reader still has to guess what they refer to.
- Be courteous without burying the instruction in padding.

## Use the human voice, not the corporate voice

Source: [Use the human voice, not the corporate voice](https://gilest.org/notes/human-voice.html), 16 October 2015.

The essay distinguishes a natural human voice from the formal voice people adopt when writing for an organisation. Official-sounding language can make a message harder to read, or discourage people from reading it at all. Sounding official is not the same as communicating successfully.

Its alternative is to write as one person speaking to another. Serious subjects do not require a stiff voice, and clarity does not make the writer less informed or credible.

Apply this to agent messages:

- Do not turn a status update into an official notice. “The tests passed” is enough; “Please be advised that testing has been completed successfully” adds no useful meaning.
- Keep the reader’s situation and the seriousness of the subject in mind. Human does not mean jokey, overfamiliar, or artificially cheerful.
- Ask whether you would actually say the sentence to the person, face to face.

## Our adaptation

The essays support conversational clarity and tone. This skill applies those ideas to agent messages, including naming projects and files explicitly and using technical vocabulary already shared with the reader. Those are our applications, not rules quoted from the essays.
